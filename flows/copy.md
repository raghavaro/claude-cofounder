# The copy

Five steps, roughly 25 minutes. Output is the words a page is built from: a
headline, one action, three reasons to believe, the objection that stops people,
and the voice rules that keep them consistent.

**This is the short run.** `marketing-workshop`'s copy flow is the fuller
version — nine steps, a second asset, keyword and on-page work, AI-answer
visibility. This module exists so the workshop finishes without needing it.
Where there's time and that skill is installed, say so and use it instead.

Nothing here is designed yet. The page comes next; this step decides what it
says.

Run one step per turn. See the pacing section in `SKILL.md`.

---

## Step 1 — The headline

Read the brief first — the sentence, the one word, the segment, the ICP's
person and trigger. The headline is a compression of those, not a new idea.

**Ask them to draft it first.** One line, then tighten together. A headline
someone wrote is one they'll defend on Thursday; a headline handed to them gets
replaced by the first person who dislikes it.

Once they've drafted, offer three shapes and have them pick — writing one of
each, not describing them:

1. **Plain** — what it is, for whom. Low ceiling, no downside.
2. **The trigger** — the moment from the ICP, named. Strong when the trigger is
   specific enough to be recognised.
3. **Against the alternative** — pointed at whatever the brief named as the
   thing people do instead. Highest recall, only works if that alternative is
   genuinely what they'd otherwise use.

**Push back on:** a headline that could belong to anyone in the category.

*They say:* "Smarter invoicing for modern teams."
*Weak:* "Should we say 'modern businesses' instead of 'modern teams'?" — a real
question about the wrong half; both versions belong to any company in the
category.
*Better:* "Cover your logo and show that line to someone. Could they name three
companies it might be? Your one word is 'hourly' — nothing in this sentence
says hourly."

**Write to brief:** Headline (header), plus the two shapes they rejected — those
matter later when someone asks what was considered.

## Step 2 — The one action

**Ask:** What is the single thing you want a visitor to do?

One action. A page offering three equal buttons is a page that hasn't decided,
and it converts like one. The action follows from the stage: buy, book a call,
request access, join a waitlist, install.

**Then ask the half people skip:** what happens immediately after they do it —
honestly. If the real answer
is "I get an email and reply when I can", put that on the page. "I'll reply
within a day" outperforms an implied automated flow that doesn't exist, and it
is the one promise a pre-launch page can definitely keep.

Name the button. The words on it, not the concept — "Start free trial" and "Get
started" are different buttons.

**Write to brief:** the action, the button text, and what happens after it.

---

## Step 3 — Three reasons to believe

**Ask:** Why should someone believe this works?

Three, ranked. Ranked because everything below the first gets cut when space
runs out, and because an unranked list is what makes a page read as noise.

Each needs evidence sitting next to it. **With no customers yet**, what counts:
a demonstration or screenshot of the thing working, a mechanism described
concretely enough to be checked, a design partner's own words, the founder's
relevant track record. What doesn't: adjectives standing in for proof, and any
number that hasn't happened yet.

**Never invent a number.** Not a percentage, not a time saved, not a customer
count. A made-up figure on a launch page is the one mistake that can't be walked
back once someone quotes it.

**Write to brief:** the three ranked reasons with their evidence, and — where
evidence is missing — which piece they'll add first.

---

## Step 4 — The objection

**Ask:** What's the last thing someone thinks before deciding not to act?

One or two, not five. A page that pre-empts every doubt raises doubts nobody
had. The ICP's disqualifiers help here: the objections worth answering come from
people inside the profile, not from the ones already ruled out.

The common ones for a new product, and what actually answers them:

- **"Never heard of you."** A name and a face, not corporate language.
- **"Is this real yet?"** Say what stage it's at. An honest "early access, three
  teams using it" beats implying scale.
- **"What if it doesn't work for me?"** Make leaving cheap — no card, short
  commitment, an export.

Answers, not deflections. If the objection is "you're too new", the page can't
claim to be established; it has to make new acceptable.

**Write to brief:** the objection and its answer, paired.

## Step 5 — Voice

The express positioning pass skipped voice rules, so this module makes them —
quickly, because the page needs them in five minutes.

**Ask:** Name two brands you'd want to sound like, and one you'd hate to sound
like.

Comparisons beat adjectives. "Professional but approachable" means nothing;
"like Basecamp, not like Salesforce" is actionable.

Convert to rules that can be followed:

- Three things this voice does — short sentences; concrete nouns; admits limits
- Three things it never does — exclamation marks; "revolutionary"; hedging
- One before/after: take the headline from step 1 and rewrite it in voice

**Push back on:** a voice borrowed from a company selling to a different person.

*They say:* "Stripe and Linear."
*Weak:* "Good — clean and technical, that's a strong direction." — it is, for
developers. The ICP might be agency owners, who read that register as cold.
*Better:* "Both of those sell to engineers. Your person is an agency owner
between client calls. Which of those two would still land with them, and what
would you have to change about it?"

**Write to brief:** the voice rules and the before/after.

## Closing the module

Read back the headline and the button. Nothing else — the brief holds the rest.

Then the checkpoint: ask them to post their headline in the chat.

**Then what's next.** The landing page module assembles this into a page and
ships it — section order, the states nobody remembers, and either publishing it
or producing paste-ready copy with a build checklist.

## If they already have a page

Audit before writing. Read their current headline against the brief and say in
three lines what it gets wrong: does it name the segment, is the one word
anywhere on it, how many claims carry evidence. Then run the module normally —
the audit makes the rewrite obvious rather than arbitrary, and takes two minutes.
