# The ICP

Five steps, roughly 25 minutes. Output is a profile specific enough to filter a
list with and to disqualify against — not another description of the customer.

**Positioning decided which group. This module makes that group findable.** If
what comes out could be satisfied by any company in the segment, the module
failed: it restated positioning in more words.

The test to hold throughout: *could someone who has never met this business use
this profile to sort a hundred companies into yes, no and maybe?* If not, keep
going.

**Suggested reading:** *Competing Against Luck*, Clayton Christensen — or
Bob Moesta's *Demand-Side Sales 101* for the more tactical version.

**Say what this is before you start.** This module runs on Jobs to Be
Done: a "job" is the progress someone is trying to make in a given
circumstance, and people don't buy products, they hire them to make that
progress. Three of the five steps map onto it directly:

- **The circumstance** — Step 1, the account: the situational facts a
  stranger could verify from outside, which is what a "job" is always
  defined relative to.
- **The struggling moment** — Step 3, the trigger, plus the question it was
  missing: what happens to the account if that moment passes and nothing
  changes. JTBD calls the forces at that moment push, pull, anxiety, and
  habit; a trigger with no stated cost is missing the push.
- **The switch interview** — Step 5, testing the profile against people who
  exist. JTBD's own method is built on interviewing people who recently
  switched to reconstruct exactly this timeline.

**Step 2 (who signs, who can block) and Step 4 (disqualifiers) aren't JTBD
vocabulary** — they're this module's own addition, because a B2B buying
committee and an explicit refusal list are real and JTBD doesn't cover
either. Say that plainly too, rather than forcing every step into one
book's terms: three steps borrow the theory, two are the module's own.

Run one step per turn. See the pacing section in `SKILL.md`.

## Step 0 — Read what positioning decided

Before asking anything, read `company-brief.md` and say back:

- **Who it's for** — the segment. This module specifies it; it does not revisit
  it. If it looks wrong, say so once and ask whether to proceed or go back, per
  the rule in `SKILL.md` about not rewriting an earlier module's decision.
- **The excluded group** — whoever positioning said they'd turn away. This is
  the seed of step 4 and the most valuable thing positioning hands over.
- **The problem and the hole** — what the profile has to be consistent with.

**If the brief has no excluded group**, don't proceed as if it's there. Ask
once, now: *who would you turn away today?* One named group is enough, and
getting it here is cheaper than discovering in step 4 that nothing was ever
ruled out. Positioning usually supplies it — its breadth push asks for exactly
this — so a brief without one means that push didn't land, which is worth
noticing rather than quietly patching.

## Step 1 — The account

**Ask:** Describe the kind of organisation this is bought by — not who could
buy, who *should*.

Get the attributes that can actually be checked from outside:

- Size — headcount, revenue, or the unit that matters in their world (seats,
  locations, monthly orders, students)
- Industry or vertical, if it's genuinely load-bearing rather than decorative
- Stage or maturity — funded, bootstrapped, established, post-acquisition
- The tooling or platform they're already on, where it determines fit
- Geography, only where it changes something: language, regulation, timezone

**Selling to individuals rather than organisations?** Replace this with the
person's *situation* — what they're in the middle of, what they already pay for,
what they've tried. A situation is checkable; a demographic mostly isn't.

**Push back on:** ranges so wide they exclude nobody.

*They say:* "Companies with 10 to 500 employees."
*Weak:* "Which end of that range converts best?" — a sensible question that
accepts a 50× range as a starting point and narrows it to a 10× one.
*Better:* "That's most companies. At 10 people this is someone's side task; at
500 there's a team who owns it and a procurement process. Which of those two
businesses are you actually built for — and what breaks about your product at
the other end?"

**Push back on:** industry named out of habit rather than fit. Ask what
specifically is different about that industry's version of the problem. If
nothing is, it isn't an ICP attribute.

**Write to brief:** the account attributes, in the module's section.

## Step 2 — The person, and the rest of the room

**Ask:** Who feels the problem?

Then, once that has an answer: who has to say yes? Often not the same person,
and conflating them is why a lot of copy addresses nobody. Between the two,
get:

- **The person with the problem** — their role, what they're accountable for,
  and what they're judged on at the end of the quarter
- **Who signs** — and whether that's the same person or someone who has to be
  convinced second-hand
- **Who can block it** — security, legal, IT, an incumbent vendor's champion,
  a partner who has to agree

Then the useful one: **what does the person with the problem have to say
internally to get this bought?** That sentence is what the landing page has to
arm them with, and it's usually not the sentence the founder would write.

**Write to brief:** the roles, and the internal sentence.

---

## Step 3 — The trigger

**Ask:** What happens immediately before someone goes looking for this?

This is the highest-value field in the profile and the one most often skipped.
A profile without it describes who *could* buy at any time, which is why
targeting built from it feels like shouting at a category.

A trigger is an **event with a date**, not a state:

- New: a hire, a funding round, a system migration, a regulation taking effect,
  a contract renewal window, losing a big customer, a location opening
- Not: "they're growing", "they care about efficiency", "they're
  data-driven" — those are always true of somebody and never true on a Tuesday

**Push back on:** a state dressed as a trigger.

*They say:* "The trigger is when they realise their current process doesn't
scale."
*Weak:* "How do they realise that?" — reasonable, and it produces another
paragraph of interior monologue you can't target on.
*Better:* "That's a realisation, not an event. What happened the week before
they realised it? Somebody got hired, something broke, a number crossed a line —
what was it, for the last customer you can actually remember?"

**Then the implication, the step a trigger alone skips: what happens to this
account if the trigger passes and nothing changes?** Not what happens if the
problem exists in the abstract — what happens *to this specific account* in
the weeks after the trigger, if they don't act on it. A trigger with no
implication is a date with nothing riding on it, and it's the reason
targeting built from a trigger alone still gets ignored.

*They say:* "If they don't fix it, I guess it stays annoying."
*Weak:* "How annoying?" — asks for an intensity rating, not a consequence.
*Better:* "Annoying to who, and what does it cost them specifically — a
missed number, a customer who leaves, a person who quits? If the honest
answer is 'nothing much,' the trigger isn't load-bearing yet."

**Write to brief:** two or three triggers, each an event someone else could
observe, paired with the implication for each.

## Step 4 — The disqualifiers

**Ask:** Who is a bad fit even though they'd probably pay?

Start from positioning's excluded group and make it operational. Then add the
ones positioning wouldn't have caught:

- Too small to be worth serving, or too large to serve without changing the
  product
- Needs something you've decided not to build
- Wrong regulatory or compliance regime
- Buys through a process you can't survive — a nine-month procurement cycle, a
  reseller you don't have
- Has an incumbent whose contract has two years to run

**Say the cost out loud.** Every disqualifier is revenue being refused, and a
founder who won't name one hasn't chosen a customer — they've described a
market. If the list is empty, the ICP is a description of everybody with a
credit card, and step 5 will show it.

**Write to brief:** the disqualifiers, with a one-line reason each.

---

## Step 5 — Test it against people who exist

**Ask:** Take your last five customers — or, with none yet, the last five real
conversations from the base module — and score each against the profile.

For each: does it match on account, person, trigger, and does it fail any
disqualifier?

Then read the result honestly. Three outcomes, and only one is comfortable:

- **Your best customers score high.** The profile is describing something real.
- **Your best customer fails the profile.** The profile is wrong, not the
  customer. Find which attribute excluded them and ask whether it was ever load
  bearing.
- **Everyone scores high, including the ones you regret.** The profile isn't
  filtering. Go back to step 4 — the disqualifiers are too soft.

**Push back on:** rationalising an exception.

*They say:* "They don't really fit the profile but they're our best account, so
they're just a special case."
*Weak:* "Fair — every ICP has outliers." — true in general, and here it's the
sentence that turns a failed test into a passed one.
*Better:* "One of two things is true: they're an outlier you can't get more of,
or the profile is missing the attribute that actually made them work. Which is
it? If you'd take ten more like them, it's the second."

**Never invent the scoring data.** If they can't remember what triggered a
deal, that's a finding — write it down as unknown rather than filling it in.

**Write to brief:** ICP (header) as one line — the account, the person, and the
trigger in a sentence. The full profile goes in its own draft, `icp.md`, and the
brief points at it.

## Closing the module

Read back the one-line ICP and the top disqualifier. Nothing else.

Then the checkpoint: ask them to post their ICP line and the group they're
refusing in the chat. The refusal is the interesting half — a set of ICPs with no
disqualifiers is a group that hasn't chosen yet.

**Then what's next.** The ICP is an input to two things and it's worth naming
both so nobody expects this module to have done them. The landing page speaks to
the person from step 2 and names the trigger from step 3. And where to actually
reach them — communities, directories, newsletters, events — is the distribution
work in `marketing-workshop`, which reads the segment and this profile. **This
module does not build a list of companies or people, and does not do outreach.**
That's out of scope here and there; say so plainly if asked rather than
producing one.

## Starting from nothing

With no customers, four of the five steps run unchanged — an ICP is a decision
about who to serve, and a founder can make it before anyone has paid. Two
things differ:

- **Step 3's trigger is a hypothesis, not a memory.** Say so, and write it as a
  prediction with a way to check it: the next five conversations either show
  the trigger or they don't.
- **Step 5 uses the conversations from the base module** rather than customers.
  Five real conversations is a weaker test than five accounts and a much better
  one than none. If there aren't five conversations either, the profile is
  entirely untested — write that in the brief in one line, because the next
  person reading it will otherwise treat it as a finding.
